Documentation 01 - Brand brief

Brand brief

What Starship Suits is, who it is for, how the competition positions itself, and the voice and naming decisions that follow. Everything in the visual directions document traces back to a decision made here.

The business

Starship Suits is a fursuit-making studio. It builds custom character costumes to commission: one client, one character, one suit at a time, made to measure. The two studios given as reference points show the shape of the market clearly.

This is a high-consideration, low-volume, high-value business. Heckhoundz Studio prices fullsuits between $6,500 and $12,000 and opens a handful of slots a year. A buyer at that price point does not impulse-purchase; they research for months, follow the maker's work, and wait for a slot to open. That single fact should drive the entire site.

The site's actual job It is not a shop. It is a portfolio that converts patience into a booked slot. Its three real jobs, in order: prove craft quality through photography, set price expectations honestly before anyone enquires, and make it obvious when the next slot opens and how to claim it.

Audience

The commissioner

Has a character they have designed or had designed, has been saving for a year or more, and is comparing four or five makers. They want to know: can this maker execute my character, what will it cost, when can I get one, and will they communicate during the build. Mostly 18-35, fandom-fluent, on Bluesky, Telegram and Instagram.

The browser

Not buying now, possibly buying in two years. They follow the work, share it, and are the reason a slot sells out in an hour when it opens. The site should be pleasant to revisit and easy to share - which makes the portfolio and the social links matter more than any call to action.

The convention or press contact

Small but valuable. Wants a clear studio identity, a way to make contact, and images they can use. A clean About and Contact page serves them entirely.

Competitive positioning

The two reference studios sit at opposite ends of the same market. Reading them together shows where the gap is.

Kemono style only - "big eyes and big proportions". Four tiers, nothing else. Either is viable. Specialist commands higher prices and is easier to market; generalist earns sooner and smooths income between slots.
Pricing On a dedicated page, broken down by part. Public ranges, $3,000-$12,000, varying by character complexity and colour count. Publish ranges. Both competitors do, and it filters out unqualified enquiries before they cost time.

Quotes Contact by email, phone, WhatsApp. Explicitly "I do not offer quotes". A structured enquiry form beats an email address: it collects the character reference, tier and budget up front instead of over six messages.
Platform Wix, free tier - no custom domain. Wix, paid, custom domain. See document 05. Both are template sites; a well-built custom site is an immediate credibility signal in a craft business.

What this adds up to

The market's weak spot is not visual - it is operational transparency. Every studio shows pretty suits. Almost none tell you where you are in the queue, what you are agreeing to, or what happens after you pay a deposit. Starship Suits can own that, and the space theme supports it naturally: mission status, flight manifest, launch window. The metaphor does real work rather than just decorating.

Name and wordmark

The organisation is Starship Suits (two words). The domain and repository are starshipsuits (one word).

The pun is the asset "Starship" and "suits" collide on the space-suit reading, and the site's whole reason for existing is that these are not space suits. Lean into it. The wordmark should let the two halves be visually separable - one weight or colour change at the seam - so the joke reads at a glance and the halves can be split apart in a logo, favicon, or badge.

Brand attributes

Three words the work should feel like, and their opposites - the second column is the more useful one, because it tells you what to reject.

IsIs notWhat that means in practice
EngineeredSlapdash, mysterious Show construction: patterns, foam, build stages. Talk in specifications - fan count, vision type, padding style. Treat a suit as built, not conjured.
WarmCold, corporate, aloof A real person's voice. Space here means the optimistic, hand-painted era, not sterile sci-fi minimalism. The customer is excited; meet them there.

Voice

Plain, specific, and quietly funny. The space metaphor is a spice, not the meal - one or two touches per page, dropped entirely wherever clarity matters more.

Write this

"Fullsuits take about four months. You'll get photos at the foam, fur, and finish stages - and you can chase me any time in between."

Specific, honest, reassuring about the thing buyers actually worry about: silence after payment.

Not this

"Embark on an interstellar journey of self-expression as our master artisans forge your cosmic alter-ego across the fabric of the galaxy."

Says nothing. Costs trust. The metaphor has eaten the message.

Terminology

Use the fandom's own vocabulary without explaining it - the audience already knows it, and explaining it signals an outsider. Reserve plain language for the commercial terms, where ambiguity costs money.

Native: partial, fullsuit, digitigrade, plantigrade, DTD (duct tape dummy), head base, follow-me eyes, kemono, toony, ref sheet. Always plain: deposit, balance, payment plan, turnaround, slot, refund.

Colour rationale

Orange, pink and a light neutral were specified. They are a good fit, and worth understanding rather than just applying:

  • Orange carries the space reference honestly - flight-suit orange, re-entry heat, mission-patch print. It is warm rather than clinical, which matches the craft.
  • Pink keeps it from becoming a NASA pastiche and reads as fandom-native rather than aerospace-corporate. It is also the softer of the two, which suits a plush product.
  • Light neutral is doing the most important job: it is the background that suit photography sits on. Fursuits are loud, saturated objects. The brand's job is to frame them, not compete with them.

The consequence, and it applies to all three visual directions: orange and pink are accents, not fields. The moment they cover large areas, every photograph on the page fights them.

What is still unknown

Answering these sharpens the brief considerably; none of them block the visual-direction choice.

  • Specialist or generalist? Does the studio also want art commissions, merch, and refurb work?
  • A house style - kemono, toony, realistic - or open to any character?
  • Real tiers and prices. Every figure in these documents is benchmarked from Heckhoundz, not the studio's own.
  • Capacity: how many slots per year, and what is a realistic turnaround?
  • Existing finished work available to photograph.
